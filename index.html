Anatomy of
a Heavenly Bed

  • PureWeave™ Stretch-Knit Cover with Tencel

    A four-way stretch knit cover woven from Tencel lyocell fibres — naturally moisture-wicking, breathable, and soft to the touch. In the Signature and Reserve, copper-infused yarns assist with heat dissipation and surface hygiene.

  • Gel-Infused Memory Foam Comfort Layer

    Open-cell gel memory foam that conforms to the body's topography and distributes pressure across a broad surface area. The gel infusion draws warmth through the foam rather than allowing it to accumulate at the surface.

  • Talalay Latex Contour Layer

    Talalay-processed natural latex offers a responsive, springy feel that neither traps you nor ignores you. Its open-cell structure promotes airflow, and its natural origin means it resists dust mites and allergens without chemical treatment.

  • Heavenly Coil System™ — Individually Encased, Zoned

    Each coil sits within its own fabric pocket, moving only in response to the weight directly above it. Depending on the model, the system is zoned across three or five regions, with firmer coils supporting the lumbar zone and softer ones beneath the shoulders and legs.

  • High-Density Foam-Encased Edge Support

    A continuous perimeter of high-density foam holds the edge of the mattress firm under load, extending the usable sleeping surface to the very border and ensuring the mattress does not yield when you sit on it each morning.

  • Reinforced Base Support Layer

    A dense, stable foam foundation anchors the entire construction, preventing migration between layers and providing the structural rigidity that allows everything above it to function as designed over the full term of the warranty.

The Heavenly Guide

7 Steps to Buying the Best Mattress for You

Buying a mattress well requires knowing what questions to ask. This guide will take you through the considerations that most people miss — and some that the industry would rather you didn't think about.

01

Understand how you sleep

Sleep position is the single most determinative factor in mattress selection, and yet most people choose a mattress based on how it feels when they sit on the edge of the display model. Side sleepers need surface yield at the shoulder and hip — areas that, when compressed against a firm surface, generate the kind of persistent pressure point that wakes you without quite waking you. Back and stomach sleepers need the inverse: a surface that holds the spine in its natural curve without allowing the pelvis to sink.

Temperature and partnership matter too. If you regularly wake warm, the surface and internal materials need to work actively against heat accumulation — copper-infused fibres, open-cell latex, and gel foam all serve this purpose in different ways. If you share a bed, motion isolation — the degree to which one person's movement is felt by the other — becomes a real consideration, not a marketing phrase. Individually encased coil systems perform significantly better in this regard than continuous coil or open-spring systems.

02

Know your body

Weight distribution and pressure points are not the same thing, and most mattress descriptions conflate them. Heavier individuals place more load on the support core — a mattress with a lower coil count and less edge reinforcement will fatigue more quickly and develop uneven compression over time. But weight distribution is also about geometry: a side sleeper with broader hips relative to their shoulders needs more hip give than a side sleeper with a more linear silhouette.

Back and joint considerations should influence your choice significantly. If you wake with lower back stiffness, you almost certainly need more lumbar-specific support — which is what zoned coil systems provide. If you have hip or shoulder pain, you need a comfort layer (foam, latex, or quilt) thick and responsive enough to redistribute pressure away from those points. This is not a question of firmness preference; it is a question of structural requirement.

03

Decode the materials

Innerspring mattresses — continuous or open-coil — are low-cost, high-airflow, and short-lived. They transmit movement readily and provide little surface contouring. Foam mattresses (memory foam, polyfoam) offer pressure relief and motion isolation but can trap heat and, in lower-density variants, compress unevenly over time. Latex, whether Talalay or Dunlop processed, offers a springier, cooler alternative with natural antimicrobial properties and exceptional durability.

Hybrids — which combine an encased coil support core with foam, latex, or natural fibre comfort layers — represent the current standard for premium mattresses because they allow each material to do what it does best: coils provide support, airflow, and edge strength; foam and latex provide contouring and pressure relief; natural fibres regulate temperature and add a quality of softness that synthetic materials cannot approximate. The Heavenly Collection is built on this architecture throughout.

04

Look past coil counts

Coil count matters — to a point. More coils, in a given area, generally means finer-grained response to weight distribution. But coil count figures are also routinely inflated by manufacturers using smaller-gauge coils that contribute less structural support. The more meaningful question is whether the coil system is individually encased (pocketed), and whether it is zoned.

Individually encased coils respond independently to the weight directly above them, rather than as a connected system that transmits force laterally. Zoning means different sections of the coil system are calibrated to different loads — firmer beneath the lumbar region, where most body weight concentrates in a back sleeper, and more yielding beneath the shoulders and legs. The Signature's three-zone ContourCore™ system delivers this differentiation; the Reserve's five-zone system does so at greater resolution.

05

Cooling and breathability

Body temperature drops naturally during sleep onset, and a mattress surface that works against this — retaining heat at the contact layer — disturbs the physiological process without the sleeper ever knowing why. Copper-infused surface fibres conduct heat away from the skin at the point of contact. Gel memory foam disperses heat through the foam matrix rather than accumulating it. Open-cell latex, by its cellular structure, allows air to move through the material rather than trapping warmth within it.

The airflow properties of an encased coil system are also significant — the coil chamber below the comfort layers acts as a passive ventilation channel that continuous foam cores cannot replicate. If you sleep hot, look for a combination of these factors rather than any single one: cover technology, gel comfort layers, open-cell foam or latex, and a coil system that allows warm air to move out of the mattress rather than cycling back through it.

06

Edge support, motion isolation, and longevity

Edge support is the difference between a mattress you can use to its full width and one that effectively has a six-inch perimeter of unusable soft territory. Foam-encased edges — a perimeter wall of high-density foam surrounding the coil system — hold their shape under load and allow you to sit on the edge of the bed without it yielding dramatically. Portrait-encased edges, as in the Reserve, provide this support across the full profile depth of the mattress.

Motion isolation is the degree to which movement on one side of the bed is felt on the other. Individually encased coil systems consistently outperform open-spring and continuous coil systems in this regard, as each coil's response is contained. Longevity is partly a materials question (latex outlasts foam; higher-density foam outlasts lower-density foam) and partly a warranty question — a meaningful warranty is not a marketing gesture, it is a signal of a manufacturer's confidence in their construction tolerances.

07

Trial periods, warranties, and testing in store

A mattress reveals itself slowly. The first night tells you about obvious comfort; the first week tells you about pressure points; the first month tells you whether the surface is beginning to hold the shape of your body in the way it should, and whether the support core is holding its geometry. Any meaningful trial period should be at least 60 nights, and ideally 100. Be cautious of short trial periods — they are structured so the mattress hasn't yet had time to demonstrate its limitations.

When testing in store, lie in your actual sleep position — not on your back for thirty seconds. Spend five to ten minutes in position, in quiet, ideally with your partner doing the same beside you. Notice whether your spine feels level, whether your shoulder has sunk to a comfortable depth, whether you feel heat building at the contact surface. A mattress that feels good at rest will feel even better after a decade of use, if it was built to last. One that feels only adequate in the showroom will feel less so a year from now.

How we sleep

Rituals for a Better Night

The bed is only part of the picture. These are the practices that frame it — small acts of intention that compound, quietly, into the quality of your days.

◷

The consistent hour

Your circadian rhythm is not a preference; it is a biological system that operates on pattern. Going to bed and rising at the same time each day — including weekends — anchors this system more effectively than any other single intervention. Within a fortnight, most people find they no longer need an alarm. Within a month, they notice the difference in the quality of the sleep they were getting before.

◑

Light, managed

Bright overhead lighting in the hour before bed suppresses melatonin release with surprising efficiency. Shift to warm, low, indirect sources — a lamp rather than an overheard light, candlelight where the mood allows. Your body reads light temperature and intensity as a signal about the time of day. Give it a signal that says evening, and it will respond accordingly.

❄

The 16–19°C room

Core body temperature drops by approximately one degree Celsius during sleep onset. A room between 16 and 19 degrees Celsius facilitates this drop; a warmer room slows it. If a cooler room is not possible, lightweight natural fibre bedding — linen, cotton percale, or Tencel — draws moisture and heat from the body rather than insulating it back. Your mattress cover does some of this work, but the ambient environment does more.

☕

The cut-off point

Caffeine has a half-life of approximately five to six hours in most adults. A coffee at three in the afternoon is a quarter-strength coffee at eleven at night — present, and disrupting. The same applies to alcohol, which induces drowsiness but fragments the second half of the sleep cycle with a consistency that most regular drinkers have simply normalised. Neither of these facts requires you to change your life. They are worth knowing.

✦

The writing habit

Intrusive thought at bedtime — the inventory of tomorrow's tasks, the replaying of today's conversation — is partially a function of the mind having no other container for these items. A brief writing practice at the end of the day gives them one. This doesn't need to be journalling in any meaningful sense: a list of what is unresolved, what was accomplished, and what tomorrow holds is often enough to release the mind's grip on it.

◻

The quiet bedroom

The bedroom as a dedicated sleep environment — free of work materials, screens, and accumulative clutter — trains the brain to associate the space with rest rather than stimulation. This is less about minimalism as an aesthetic and more about signal clarity. The fewer competing associations a room holds, the more reliably it delivers the one you actually want from it. A made bed in a calm room is a permission to rest.

◈

Weight and natural fibre

Weighted blankets — typically six to ten kilograms, or approximately ten percent of body weight — have reasonable evidence behind them for reducing sleep onset time and increasing subjective sleep quality. Pairing a weighted layer with natural fibres (linen, cotton, Tencel) that breathe rather than trap heat addresses the primary reason people find them too warm. The weight grounds the nervous system; the fibre keeps the surface from becoming a problem.

☀

Morning light, first

Ten to fifteen minutes of natural light exposure within the first hour of waking — ideally outside, or near a window — sets the body's circadian anchor for the day and, by extension, for the following night. It is the most reliable single intervention for improving sleep timing across populations, and the cheapest. It is also among the most consistently skipped. The quality of tonight's sleep begins with this morning's light.
